## Summary

A membership inference attack (MIA) decides whether a specific data point was in a model's training set, using only the model's behaviour. Against generative models it is the sharpest privacy probe: it audits unauthorized use of copyrighted or sensitive images, and it is the precursor and filter for outright training-data extraction {2023_Carlini}. This node collects the theory and practice of MIA on diffusion and flow-matching image (and audio) models, where the corpus is now concentrated. The organizing facts are that the attack signal comes from *memorization* — members are reconstructed, denoised, or scored more accurately than held-out data ([[diffusion_geometry]]) — that this signal concentrates at *intermediate* noising timesteps, and that attacks are ranked by model access (white-box gradients → gray-box denoiser queries → black-box API) and by how faithfully they exploit the diffusion objective rather than a naive loss.

## Open Questions

Whether diffusion models are "vulnerable" has no single answer: naive loss-threshold attacks find them *comparably resistant* to GANs {2023_Matsumoto}, yet objective-tailored attacks infer membership almost perfectly {2023_Duan}{2024_Pang_WhiteBox} and extraction shows they leak more than twice the training data of GANs {2023_Carlini} — so reported vulnerability is largely an artifact of attack sophistication and evaluation protocol. Evaluation itself is unsettled: results depend heavily on the member/hold-out split being same-distribution {2023_Duan}, on realistic step-per-image ratios rather than deliberate overfitting {2024_Zhai}, and on the strict TPR-at-low-FPR metric rather than average accuracy. The clean-generalization case is the hardest open problem: large-scale text-to-image models generalize well yet still leak through *conditional* overfitting {2024_Zhai}, and flow-matching models encode exploitable "membership signals" with no visible train–val loss gap {2026_Sesmat}. No defense yet achieves a usable privacy–utility trade-off.

## Synthesis

### Problem setting, threat model, and evaluation

Membership inference is formalized as a security game: a dataset $D$ drawn from $q_\text{data}$ is split into a member set $D_M$ and a hold-out set $D_H$; the target model $f_\theta$ is trained only on $D_M$; the adversary, given a query $x$ and some access to $\theta$, must decide $x\in D_M$ {2023_Duan}. The rigorous protocol keeps $D_M$ and $D_H$ *in the same distribution*, which is much harder than the early text-to-image setup where members and non-members came from different distributions and the "attack" partly reduced to distribution classification {2022_Wu}{2023_Duan}. The theoretical foundation is that privacy risk tracks **overfitting**: Yeom et al. proved the membership advantage of a loss-threshold adversary grows with the generalization gap, in some cases directly proportionally, while also showing overfitting is *sufficient but not necessary* for successful MIA {2018_Yeom} — a caveat that the clean-generalization leakage results below make concrete. Following the classification literature, generative MIA is evaluated with attack success rate and AUC but, decisively, with **true-positive rate at very low false-positive rate** (e.g. TPR@1%FPR), because confidently exposing a few members matters more than average accuracy {2023_Duan}{2026_Rao_SimA}. MIA also underpins a graver threat: Carlini et al.'s generate-and-filter extraction pipeline flags generations that exceed a membership-scoring criterion, recovering over a thousand near-verbatim training images (faces, logos) from Stable Diffusion and Imagen, and establishing that MIA is the entry point to data reconstruction ([[training_data_extraction]]) {2023_Carlini}.

### Why diffusion models leak: memorization, timesteps, and the score

The attack signal is memorization, and its geometry is developed in [[diffusion_geometry]]: because the optimal denoiser reproduces the *empirical* score, which has peaks at each training point, a sufficiently trained model reconstructs its members exactly unless prevented by data scale, capacity limits, or early stopping {2023_Carlini}. Rao et al. make the exploit-side statement of this precise: the expected denoiser output points from a query $x^\star$ toward a kernel-weighted local mean of nearby training samples, $\mathbb{E}[\hat\epsilon_\theta(x^\star_t,t)]\propto \mu_\text{local}(x^\star)-x^\star$; for a member the local mean collapses onto $x^\star$ itself, so the **score norm** $\lVert\hat\epsilon_\theta\rVert$ is small, whereas for a non-member it is large {2026_Rao_SimA}. Since the diffusion denoiser *is* the score by construction ($\hat\epsilon_\theta\approx-\sigma_t\nabla\log p_t$), this reduces most prior test statistics to one geometric quantity — proximity to the training set read off the score. Empirically the leakage is strongly **timestep-dependent**: intermediate noise levels are the most vulnerable, not the near-data or near-noise extremes, because $x_T$ carries almost no sample information while the largest member/non-member error gap opens as $t\to 0$ {2023_Matsumoto}{2023_Hu}{2023_Duan}. Against the once-common hope that diffusion's synthetic samples could *protect* privacy, Carlini et al. found the opposite — diffusion models are the least private image models they tested, leaking over twice as much as GANs — and that existing privacy tools do not fix this without destroying quality {2023_Carlini}.

### Attacks on unconditional diffusion models: the loss → deterministic → score progression

The attack literature is a progression toward statistics that exploit the diffusion objective faithfully and cheaply. **Naive / loss-based** attacks threshold the training loss (per-timestep denoising error) or the model likelihood: Hu & Pang's loss-based attack reaches 100% TPR@0.01%FPR on a DDPM trained on 1k FFHQ faces, and their likelihood-based variant — using diffusion's tractable likelihood as the clue — reaches 71%, with both attacks weakening as the training set grows {2023_Hu}; Matsumoto et al. use the same loss-threshold idea and conclude, from it, that DDIM is only as vulnerable as a DCGAN and mainly at small training-set sizes {2023_Matsumoto}. The weakness is that the diffusion loss is *stochastic* — it depends on a randomly drawn noise — so a single query is noisy. **SecMI** removes the randomness by exploiting DDIM's deterministic reverse/denoise: its $t$-error is the round-trip posterior-estimation error at a single well-chosen intermediate step, small for members, and its threshold and neural-network variants reach AUC 0.88 and 0.95 on CIFAR-10 DDPM ($\geq$0.80 ASR, $\geq$0.85 AUC across settings) {2023_Duan}. **PIA** makes this efficient: instead of iterating DDIM, it initializes the noise with the model's own $t=0$ output $\epsilon_\theta(x_0,0)$ to get a deterministic trajectory in just two queries, matching SecMI's AUC at higher TPR@1%FPR and 5–10× the speed {2023_Kong}. **White-box** attacks then add features the loss discards: Pang et al.'s GSA uses per-layer *gradients* (subsampled and aggregated to tame their million-dimensional size), arguing different layers store different amounts of member information, and reaches near-100% ASR and AUC$\approx$1.0 on DDPM and Imagen {2024_Pang_WhiteBox}. SimA closes the loop by showing the elaborate multi-step reconstruction is unnecessary — the single-query score norm (with a 30-sample Monte-Carlo variant, SimA-MC) matches or beats these at TPR@1%FPR {2026_Rao_SimA}.

### Text-to-image and the conditional attack surface

Conditioning both widens the attack surface and raises the bar. Wu et al. ran the first text-to-image MIA, targeting sequence-to-sequence and diffusion T2I models from output images alone with quality-, reconstruction-, and faithfulness-based statistics — but under a member/non-member distribution mismatch that eases the task {2022_Wu}. The hard, realistic regime is exposed by Zhai et al.: large T2I models generalize so well on the *marginal* image distribution that marginal-overfitting attacks fail under realistic training, yet the models measurably overfit the **conditional** distribution $p(x\mid c)$ of an image given its caption. Their **CLiD** statistic targets exactly this gap — the discrepancy between the conditional and marginal likelihoods, estimated via Monte-Carlo ELBOs — and it outperforms prior indicators while resisting early stopping and data augmentation {2024_Zhai}. Zhai et al. also supply the field's central evaluation critique: prior query-based attacks "succeed" only at excessively high step-per-image ratios and collapse under realistic training or augmentation, so MIA effectiveness must be reported against a realistic training budget rather than deliberate overfitting {2024_Zhai}. A second T2I axis is *what* was trained: Pang & Wang and Li et al. separate membership in a small **fine-tuning** set (where the model memorizes and regenerates the concept) from membership in the vast **pretraining** set (where the same prompt yields diverse outputs), the latter being substantially harder {2024_Pang_BlackBox}{2025_Li_ReDiffuse} — connecting MIA to the few-shot adaptation setting of [[diffusion_model_personalization]].

### Black-box, API-only attacks

The most deployable — and most restrictive — attacks assume no internal access at all, only an image-to-image API, and read membership off a **reconstruction-similarity** signal. Pang & Wang's reconstruction-based framework scores the distance between a fine-tuned model's generation and the target image across four scenarios, reaching AUCs of 0.95/0.85/0.93 and beating prior black-box methods by ~35%, robustly even when the shadow model's architecture is mismatched {2024_Pang_BlackBox}. Li et al.'s **ReDiffuse** needs only an image-to-image variation API: it perturbs and regenerates the query several times and averages, exploiting that a member sits in a "region of attraction" so its averaged variations stay close to the original; it also ports MIA to the Diffusion Transformer for the first time {2025_Li_ReDiffuse}. Bohacek & Farid's *GenAI Confessions* seeds an img2img pipeline with the query at increasing strengths and measures DreamSim similarity of output to seed — in-training seeds yield consistently more similar outputs — a method that is architecture-agnostic, cheap, and works on a single image; they release **STROLL**, 100 confirmed member/non-member photo pairs, as a controlled benchmark {2025_Bohacek}. Pang et al. note that what SecMI, PIA, and Hu's likelihood attack call "black-box" is really **gray-box**, since they consume intermediate denoising outputs a real API would hide — a terminology gap worth tracking (see below) {2024_Pang_BlackBox}.

### Flow matching and rectified flow: locating the signal in the interpolation path

The newest and most theoretical thread targets flow-matching / rectified-flow models ([[flow_matching]]), which underpin Stable Diffusion 3 and FLUX, and where diffusion attacks like SecMI/PIA do not transfer because there is no iterative denoising to exploit {2026_Sesmat}. Two concurrent works converge on the same mechanism. Rao & Moyer build the first non-trivial RF attack as a sequence of statistics ($T_\text{naive}\to T_\text{mc}\to$ a complexity-calibrated $T_\text{mc\_cal}$ that decouples an image's intrinsic spatial complexity from genuine memorization, gaining up to +15% AUC and +45% TPR@1%FPR), and show susceptibility peaks sharply at the integration **midpoint** $t=0.5$: there the noisy state is statistically orthogonal to the target velocity, so the network cannot rely on a linear (LMMSE) predictor and must use nonlinear capacity to memorize sample-specific detail; the whole-trajectory memorization risk is upper-bounded by its midpoint value {2026_Rao_Memorization}. Sesmat et al. reach the same picture along the linear interpolation path $X_\lambda=(1-\lambda)X_0+\lambda X_1$: the train–test reconstruction gap — which they identify as the *membership signal*, the correlation between the model's error and sample-specific residuals — follows a **bell-shaped curve over $\lambda$** that accumulates during training while validation loss stays flat, and peaks where the linear information $X_\lambda$ carries about the velocity vanishes, forcing nonlinear (memorizing) fits. Crucially they derive the peak location $\lambda^\star_F$ in closed form from the data covariances $\Sigma_0,\Sigma_1$ alone, so it is set by data geometry and independent of architecture, with model choices affecting only the peak's magnitude; the bell shape is universal across image and audio {2026_Sesmat}. Together these generalize the older empirical "intermediate timesteps leak most" observation {2023_Matsumoto} into a derived law and recast MIA as a quantitative **memorization probe** — the ability to run an MIA is itself evidence of retained training-set information, complementing the memorization dynamics of [[diffusion_geometry]].

### Defenses and the privacy–utility tension

No defense yet gives a satisfactory trade-off. The classical route is **differential privacy** ([[differential_privacy]]): training with DP-SGD reduces memorization and demonstrably blunts both loss- and reconstruction-based attacks {2023_Hu}{2024_Pang_BlackBox}, but at a large cost to sample quality that the MIA papers uniformly flag as unacceptable {2023_Hu}{2023_Carlini}. **Overfitting mitigations** — early stopping, data augmentation, deduplication — help against naive attacks but are exactly what CLiD is built to survive, since conditional overfitting persists where marginal overfitting is controlled {2024_Zhai}{2023_Carlini}. A promising training-time alternative sidesteps the DP quality hit: because RF leakage concentrates at the midpoint, replacing uniform timestep sampling with a **symmetric-exponential (U-shaped) distribution** that under-samples the vulnerable middle suppresses memorization while preserving fidelity {2026_Rao_Memorization}. Model design also matters: in audio, PIA finds mel-spectrogram (image-like) diffusion models leak while raw-waveform models are relatively robust, so the **output representation** is itself a privacy lever {2023_Kong}. Throughout, the papers stress that MIA is dual-use — the same machinery lets a model owner *audit* privacy risk and lets a data owner prove unauthorized use, aligning MIA with the copyright and provenance concerns of [[generated_image_detection]] and the data-protection motives behind [[aes_for_generative_models]] {2022_Wu}{2024_Zhai}{2025_Bohacek}.

### Terminological and evaluation hazards

Three inconsistencies recur and complicate cross-paper comparison. First, **access labels drift**: "query-based" and "black-box" in SecMI/PIA/Hu denote methods that still read intermediate denoiser outputs, which Pang et al. reclassify as *gray-box*, reserving *black-box* for output/API-only attacks {2024_Pang_BlackBox}{2025_Li_ReDiffuse}. Second, the **member/hold-out distribution** must match for a result to mean anything — cross-distribution setups inflate performance and partly measure distribution classification rather than memorization {2023_Duan}{2022_Wu}. Third, the **training budget** matters: attacks evaluated at inflated step-per-image ratios overstate real-world risk, so realistic-training and low-FPR reporting are now the expected standard {2024_Zhai}{2026_Rao_SimA}. Finally, the apparent contradiction between "diffusion is as private as a GAN" {2023_Matsumoto} and "diffusion leaks twice as much as a GAN" {2023_Carlini} dissolves once attack strength is held fixed: the former used naive loss thresholds, the latter a tailored extraction pipeline — a reminder that a negative MIA result bounds the *attack*, not the model's privacy.
